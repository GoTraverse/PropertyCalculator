How the comparison is worked out

The tool runs the same loan amount and term down two paths.

On the P&I path the monthly repayment is P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate divided by 12) and n is the number of monthly repayments (years × 12).

On the IO path the repayment during the IO period is the loan balance multiplied by the annual IO rate, divided by 12. Nothing comes off the balance, so when the IO period ends you still owe the full amount and have fewer years to repay it. A 30-year loan with a 5-year IO period is repaid over the remaining 25 years, which is why the repayment jumps. The "extra interest" figure is total interest on the IO path minus total interest on P&I from the start.

Worked example

A $600,000 loan over 30 years, with a P&I rate of 6.25%, an IO rate of 6.60% and a 5-year IO period.

  • P&I from day one is $3,694 a month for the whole 30 years.
  • Interest Only for 5 years is $600,000 × 6.60% ÷ 12 = $3,300 a month, close to $400 a month less.
  • After 5 years the balance is still $600,000 and has to be repaid over 25 years. At 6.60% that is $4,089 a month, a 24% rise on the IO repayment and more than the P&I repayment would have been.
  • Total interest is $824,644 on the IO path and $729,949 on P&I from the start, which is $94,695 more.

Who uses interest-only, and the risks

Mostly property investors. Interest on an investment loan is generally tax-deductible and principal repayments aren't, so some investors keep the investment loan on IO and put their spare cash into a non-deductible debt such as their own home loan. IO is also used to get through a temporary drop in income (parental leave, a career break) or while a new home is being built.

The main risk is the repayment after the IO period ends, as in the example above. The other is that you build no equity during the IO years, so if prices stall or fall you have a thinner buffer than someone who has been paying the loan down. On an owner-occupied loan there's no tax deduction, so IO costs you the higher rate and the extra interest with nothing coming back.

How lenders assess an IO loan

APRA expects lenders to test whether you could still afford the loan at an interest rate around 3 percentage points above the actual rate. For an IO loan that test is usually run on the P&I repayment over the term left after IO ends, which is higher than the IO repayment, so qualifying for IO is often harder than for P&I.

What this calculator leaves out

Changes to interest rates over time (it assumes the IO rate carries on after the loan reverts, and your lender may apply a different revert rate), fees and charges, Lenders Mortgage Insurance, offset or redraw balances, and tax deductions. It is general information, not personal financial advice.
