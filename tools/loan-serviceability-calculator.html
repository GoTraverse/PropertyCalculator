What loan serviceability means in Australia

Serviceability is a lender's assessment of whether you can comfortably afford the repayments on a home loan — not just today, but if interest rates rise. It is the single biggest factor that decides how much you can borrow, and it is often far more restrictive than the size of your deposit. Two households with identical incomes can be approved for very different amounts depending on their expenses, existing debts, dependents and the type of work they do.

Australian lenders do not simply lend a fixed multiple of your salary. Instead they build a monthly budget for your household, strip out tax, living costs and existing commitments, and then test whether the money left over — your "net surplus" — can cover the new mortgage at a stressed interest rate. This approach is shaped by responsible-lending obligations enforced by ASIC and by prudential standards set by APRA (the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority). Understanding how that budget is built lets you see exactly where your borrowing power is won or lost.

How lenders calculate serviceability

Most Australian lenders work through a serviceability assessment in roughly these steps, and the calculator above follows the same order in a simplified form:

  • Step 1 — Assessable income. Start with gross (pre-tax) income, then apply "haircuts" to less certain income. Base salary is usually taken at 100%, but lenders commonly count only 70–80% of overtime, bonuses, commissions and rental income because those streams are variable. Government benefits and some allowances may be counted in full, partially, or not at all.
  • Step 2 — Tax. Income tax and the Medicare levy are deducted to arrive at net income, since you repay a loan out of after-tax dollars.
  • Step 3 — Living expenses. The lender takes the higher of your declared living expenses or the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) benchmark for a household of your size and income.
  • Step 4 — Existing commitments. Repayments on car loans, personal loans, buy-now-pay-later and HECS/HELP are subtracted, along with a notional repayment on the full limit of any credit cards — even cards you never use.
  • Step 5 — The buffer. The proposed repayment is calculated not at the advertised rate but at a stressed "assessment rate" that adds APRA's serviceability buffer of around 3 percentage points.

In words, the formula is: Net surplus = (assessable income − tax) − living expenses (HEM floor) − existing debt repayments − new loan repayment at the buffered rate. If that surplus is positive, the loan is serviceable; the maximum loan is the largest amount where the surplus stays at or above zero. Lenders also apply a debt-to-income (DTI) sanity check, and many treat a total DTI above roughly 6 times gross income as higher risk requiring extra scrutiny.

How this calculator simplifies those steps. Salary is counted at 100% and other income at 75%. Tax uses the FY 2026-27 resident scale plus the Medicare levy. The expense floor is my own rough stand-in for HEM, based on household income and one or two adults, with no dependants. Credit cards are assessed at 3% of the total limit a month. The loan is tested over 30 years, principal and interest, at your rate plus 3%. It does not apply a debt-to-income cap.

Why the APRA buffer is 3%

In October 2021 APRA increased the minimum serviceability buffer banks must use from 2.5% to 3 percentage points above the loan's interest rate. The idea is to make sure borrowers can still cope if rates climb after they sign up. If your actual rate is 6.0%, the bank tests your repayments at around 9.0%. Because the buffer is applied to the whole loan, it has an outsized effect: a borrower might comfortably afford repayments at today's rate yet be declined because the stressed repayment pushes their budget into the red. The buffer is a guideline rather than a hard ceiling, and lenders may apply a higher floor rate, but you should expect roughly a 3% buffer at almost every mainstream lender.

HEM — the living-expense benchmark

The Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) is a benchmark of what a household of a given size and income typically spends on essentials and modest discretionary items. It is published quarterly by the Melbourne Institute and used across the industry as a minimum living-expense floor. If you declare expenses below the HEM figure for your circumstances, the lender substitutes HEM instead — so claiming unrealistically low spending will not boost your borrowing power. HEM scales with income and household size, which is why adding a partner or a dependent child changes your result. It is a floor, not a cap: if your genuine, verified spending is higher than HEM, the lender uses your higher figure.

Worked example

Assume a couple applying together. Partner A earns $120,000 and Partner B earns $85,000, with no other income. They have a car loan costing $650 a month and one credit card with a $10,000 limit. They enter living expenses of $4,200 a month and a loan rate of 6.0%. Entered into the calculator above, the result is:

  • Net income counted: $13,197 a month, after income tax and the Medicare levy of $28,920 and $17,720 a year.
  • Living expenses used: $4,590 a month. The expense floor for two adults on $205,000 is higher than the $4,200 entered, so the floor is used.
  • Existing commitments: $650 for the car loan, plus $300 for the credit card (3% of the $10,000 limit) even if the card is paid off in full each month.
  • Test rate: 6.0% plus the 3% buffer, so 9.0%.
  • Surplus for repayments: $13,197 − $4,590 − $650 − $300 = $7,657 a month.
  • Estimated borrowing capacity: $951,585. That is the loan $7,657 a month repays at 9.0% over 30 years. At the actual 6.0% rate the same loan would cost about $5,705 a month.

Change the card limit and the answer moves. With a $20,000 limit the estimate falls to $914,300. With a $5,000 limit it rises to $970,227.

Practical tips and common mistakes

  • Cancel or reduce unused credit cards. Lenders assess the full limit, not the balance. Dropping a $20,000 limit to $5,000 can add tens of thousands to your capacity.
  • Clear short-term debt first. A car loan or personal loan with a high monthly repayment eats into surplus far more than its balance suggests.
  • Don't understate expenses. Lenders verify spending against bank statements and apply the HEM floor, so lowballing rarely works and can delay approval.
  • Understand income haircuts. If a large share of your pay is bonus, commission or overtime, expect only part of it to count. Self-employed applicants are usually assessed on two years of tax returns.
  • Watch the DTI. Even with strong surplus, a very high debt-to-income ratio can trigger tighter scrutiny or a smaller loan.
  • Interest-only and shorter terms reduce capacity. Interest-only periods are often assessed on the higher principal-and-interest repayment that applies once the IO period ends.

What this calculator does and doesn't cover

This tool estimates borrowing capacity from five things: after-tax income, living expenses (your figure or the expense floor, whichever is higher), existing debt repayments, credit card limits and a test rate 3% above the rate you enter. Each input changes the result, so you can see what a lower card limit or a paid-off car loan is worth.

It does not copy any lender's servicing model. The expense floor is my own rough estimate, not the HEM table lenders license. It does not model dependants, shading of bonus or casual income, HECS as a separate repayment, lenders mortgage insurance, loan-to-value limits, or stamp duty and other purchase costs. Different banks use different expense tables, floor rates and policies, so two lenders can return different numbers for the same applicant. Treat the output as a planning estimate, not a pre-approval.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do I need to borrow $1 million?

It depends on your expenses, debts and the rate. With no debts or credit cards and a 6.50% loan rate, this calculator gives about $1,068,000 for a single applicant on $200,000 and about $990,000 for a couple earning $100,000 each. A car loan, a credit card limit or a higher rate lowers both figures. Run your own numbers above instead of relying on one income threshold.

Why does my credit card limit reduce my borrowing power if I pay it off?

Lenders assume you could draw the full limit at any time, so they include a notional monthly repayment based on the limit regardless of your current balance. Reducing or closing cards you don't need is one of the fastest ways to lift capacity.

Does the 3% buffer ever change?

Yes. APRA sets the minimum buffer and reviews it over time — it was 2.5% before October 2021 and has been around 3% since. Individual lenders may also apply a minimum floor rate that is higher than rate-plus-buffer. Check current settings on the APRA website (apra.gov.au) and the consumer guidance at Moneysmart (moneysmart.gov.au).

Is this the same as getting pre-approved?

No. Pre-approval involves a lender verifying your documents and credit file and is specific to one institution's policy. This calculator is a general estimate to help you understand the levers; always confirm an exact figure with a lender or mortgage broker before house-hunting.
