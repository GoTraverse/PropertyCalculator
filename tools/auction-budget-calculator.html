How the walk-away price is worked out

When the hammer falls at an Australian auction, the contract is unconditional. There's no cooling-off period and no finance or building and pest clause, and the deposit (usually 10% of the price) is due on the spot. So the limit has to be set before the day, from your own funds. The calculator works out the most you can pay, all-in, without touching your safety buffer or going past your pre-approval. It doesn't value the property.

  • Usable savings = cash savings minus the safety buffer you keep back (5% by default), for the costs that start as soon as you own the place, such as moving, small repairs and rates adjustments.
  • All-in cost = the hammer price, plus stamp duty on that price, plus title registration fees, plus conveyancing.
  • Loan = the all-in cost minus your usable savings.
  • Before LMI is added, the loan can't be more than 95% of the price. Lenders generally want a deposit of at least 5% on top of the duty, fees and conveyancing, and 5% is the smallest deposit with an ordinary loan in the First Home Journey. When your savings run out before your pre-approval does, they set the walk-away price, and the plan says how much usable savings would let you use the whole pre-approval.
  • If the loan is more than 80% of the price (the LVR), the calculator adds my estimate of lenders mortgage insurance (LMI) to it: 0.8% of the loan when the LVR is above 80%, 1.9% above 85% and 3.4% above 90%. Those rates are my own estimate, with no published rate table behind them. I've assumed the premium is added to the loan, the same as in the First Home Journey.
  • Walk-away price = the highest hammer price where the loan before LMI is no more than 95% of the price and the loan with any LMI still fits inside your pre-approval. Duty and LMI rise with the price, so the calculator finds this by repeated trial, then rounds down to the nearest $1,000.

In Queensland the duty also depends on whether you'll live in the home. An owner-occupier pays the lower home concession rate, so a "Buying to live in" box appears when you pick Queensland and haven't ticked first home buyer. The Queensland stamp duty calculator lists the conditions for the home concession. In Victoria, some owner-occupied homes valued at $550,000 or less get a principal place of residence concession. The calculator doesn't apply it, so check it with the SRO if you'll live in the home.

Worked example

Say you have $120,000 in savings, a $560,000 pre-approval, a 5% buffer and a $1,800 conveyancing estimate, and you're buying in Victoria as a non-first-home buyer. Usable savings are $114,000 (the $6,000 buffer stays put). The walk-away price comes out at $627,000. Land transfer duty is $32,690, title and registration fees are $1,701 (Victoria's transfer fee scales with the price, at $104.30 plus $2.34 per $1,000), and $1,800 of conveyancing brings the all-in total to $663,191. Your $114,000 goes toward that, so the loan is $549,191, which is 87.6% of the price. At that LVR my LMI estimate is $10,435, and adding it takes the loan to $559,626, inside the $560,000 pre-approval. A $628,000 hammer price would take the loan with LMI past $560,000.

Auctions by state

Auctions are the usual way homes sell in inner and middle-ring Melbourne and Sydney. Most Queensland homes sell by private treaty, and Queensland's five-business-day cooling-off period applies to private treaty contracts but not to auction purchases. A Queensland buyer who's used to having it can be caught out the first time they bid.

The suburb median

If you enter a suburb, the calculator shows its median next to your walk-away price. I only show figures from free public government data, and coverage is patchy:

  • Victoria: median house and unit sale prices (Valuer-General Victoria, 2025 preliminary).
  • South Australia: median weekly rents (Consumer & Business Services, Jan–Mar 2026) and median house sale prices for metro Adelaide (Valuer-General, Q1 2026).
  • Queensland: median weekly rents (Residential Tenancies Authority, March 2026). There's no free public sale-price dataset for Queensland, so there's no sale median.
  • Tasmania: median rents from bond lodgements (Department of Justice, May 2025 to Apr 2026).

New South Wales, Western Australia, the ACT and the Northern Territory aren't in the file yet, and the plan works there without a median. A median is the middle of past sales in that suburb over the stated period, not a valuation of the house you're bidding on. Every figure shows its period and source.

What it leaves out

It doesn't model what happens in the room. The auctioneer can make vendor bids for the seller, under rules that differ by state. Price guides can sit well below where a competitive auction ends. NSW and Victoria have underquoting laws. Budget from recent comparable sales instead of the guide.

It also leaves out the lender's valuation (if it comes in under your winning bid, your loan can shrink while what you owe the vendor doesn't), and buyer's agent fees, strata reports and contract review beyond the conveyancing figure you enter. The LMI figure is my estimate, and your lender's premium can differ. An eligible first home buyer pays no LMI under the 5% Deposit Scheme. The calculator doesn't check eligibility, so it counts LMI either way.
