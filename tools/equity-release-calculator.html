    What is equity release?

    Equity release means borrowing against the equity in your home — the difference between your property value and outstanding mortgage. It's commonly used to fund renovations, investment properties, or debt consolidation.

    How LVR (loan-to-value ratio) works

    LVR is the percentage of your property value you owe on the mortgage. For example, a $500,000 home with a $400,000 mortgage has an LVR of 80%.

    • Lower LVR = more equity released, easier to get approved
    • Higher LVR = less equity available, may need LMI (Lenders Mortgage Insurance)
    • Typical limits: 80% for owner-occupied homes, 70–75% for investment properties

    What affects equity release approval?

    Lenders consider multiple factors beyond just LVR:

    • Income and serviceability
    • Employment history and stability
    • Credit history and existing debts
    • Property type (residential, investment, commercial)
    • Loan structure (fixed vs. variable rate)

    LMI — When It Applies and What It Costs

    If releasing equity pushes your LVR above 80%, most lenders require Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI). LMI protects the lender (not you) if you default. Costs vary by LVR and loan amount:

    • 80–85% LVR: Roughly 0.5–1.0% of the loan amount
    • 85–90% LVR: Roughly 1.5–2.5% of the loan amount
    • Above 90%: Rarely available for equity release; 2.5–4%+ if approved

    On a $700,000 loan at 85% LVR, LMI could be $7,000–$17,500 added to your loan balance. Some lenders allow LMI to be capitalised (added to the loan) rather than paid upfront.

    Common Uses for Equity Release in Australia

    • Home renovation: Using equity to fund renovations that add value to the property
    • Investment property deposit: The most common use — releasing equity in your home to fund the deposit on an IP
    • Debt consolidation: Rolling higher-interest debts (car loans, credit cards) into a lower-rate mortgage — note this extends the repayment period
    • School fees or large expenses: Drawing on equity for major life events
    • Business purposes: Self-employed borrowers sometimes use equity as working capital (note: business-purpose loans may have different tax treatment)

    Tax Considerations

    Interest on equity released is only tax-deductible if the funds are used for income-producing purposes (e.g. a rental property deposit). Interest on equity used for personal purposes (renovation, school fees, holidays) is not deductible. Keep your loans split by purpose — mixing personal and investment borrowing in one account complicates your tax position. Speak to your accountant before releasing equity for mixed purposes.
