How it's worked out

Usable equity is the part a lender will let you borrow against, usually up to 80% of the property's value before Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI) applies, so it is almost always smaller than total equity.

  • Total equity = property value − outstanding mortgage.
  • Current LVR = outstanding mortgage ÷ property value, as a percentage.
  • Maximum borrowing at the LVR limit = property value × maximum LVR%. This is the largest total debt the lender will allow against the property.
  • Available to release = maximum borrowing − outstanding mortgage.

Worked example

A home valued at $650,000 with an outstanding mortgage of $400,000 and a lender willing to lend up to 80% LVR.

  • Total equity = $650,000 − $400,000 = $250,000.
  • Current LVR = $400,000 ÷ $650,000 = 61.5%.
  • Maximum borrowing at 80% = $650,000 × 0.80 = $520,000.
  • Available to release = $520,000 − $400,000 = $120,000.

What the figure leaves out

  • Whether you can afford the bigger loan. Since APRA introduced its serviceability buffer, lenders assess whether you could still repay at an interest rate around 3 percentage points above the actual rate.
  • The lender's valuation. Lenders order their own, and a lower figure than yours shrinks the usable equity.
  • The cost of LMI. Above 80% LVR you'll generally pay it, and it protects the lender, not you, if you default. The premium rises with LVR and varies by insurer, loan size and state. It can usually be added to the loan.

It also leaves out stamp duty, refinancing and valuation fees, your credit history and the lender's own policy. This page is general information, not personal financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How much equity can I release?

Generally up to 80% of your property's value minus your current loan, before LMI applies. Above 80% you can sometimes borrow more by paying LMI, but lenders rarely release equity above 90% LVR. The amount also has to pass the lender's income and serviceability test, so the figure here is a ceiling.

Is the interest on released equity tax-deductible?

It depends on what the borrowed money is used for. What it is secured against doesn't matter. If you use the funds to buy an income-producing asset such as a rental property or shares, the interest is generally deductible. If you use them for personal purposes such as a holiday or renovating your own home, it isn't.

What's the difference between redraw and equity release?

Redraw lets you take back extra repayments you have already made. It is your own money and usually needs no new application. Equity release is new borrowing against the value of your home, which means a new loan assessment, possibly a valuation, and LMI if it pushes your LVR above 80%.
