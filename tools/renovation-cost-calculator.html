How the estimate is built

  • Base cost = floor area (m²) × a rate per m² that depends on the scope and finish you choose.
  • Bathrooms and the kitchen (a refresh or a full replacement) are added on top as fixed allowances for your finish level, because plumbing, waterproofing, tiling and cabinetry make them cost far more than their floor area suggests.
  • Landscaping is another fixed allowance, added if you tick it.
  • Contingency = subtotal × 15%, when the box is ticked. It covers what you can't see until walls come off: rotten subfloors, undersized switchboards, old plumbing that fails inspection, or asbestos in a pre-1990 home.

Each rate has a low and a high figure, so the result is a range. The mid-point is the average of the two, and cost per m² is the mid-point divided by your floor area.

Worked example

A 180 m² house, a mid-range renovation at a mid-range finish, one bathroom, a full kitchen replacement, the 15% contingency and no landscaping. These are the calculator's default inputs.

  • Base works: 180 m² at $1,000 to $1,600 per m² = $180,000 to $288,000.
  • Bathroom (one, mid-range): $15,000 to $28,000.
  • Kitchen (full replacement, mid-range): $20,000 to $45,000.
  • Subtotal: $215,000 to $361,000.
  • Contingency at 15%: $32,250 to $54,150.
  • Total: $247,250 to $415,150. The mid-point is $331,200, or $1,840 per m².

The room allowances are added on top of the per-m² base rate, with no deduction for the floor area the kitchen and bathroom take up, so the range leans high. It's wide because the rates are rough, but it's enough to tell whether a project is worth getting quotes for.

What the estimate leaves out

It doesn't know the condition of your property, prices in your suburb, site access and slope, or heritage overlays, and it leaves out finance. It also leaves out these costs (the dollar ranges are my rough estimates, not sourced figures):

  • Council approvals / DA: $2,000–$15,000+ depending on scope
  • Structural engineer report: $1,500–$4,000
  • Asbestos testing and licensed removal, common in pre-1990 homes
  • Temporary accommodation if you need to vacate during works
  • Holding costs: mortgage interest, council rates and insurance during the build period
  • Skip bins, site cleaning and waste levies

It isn't a quote or a valuation, and it can't tell you whether the work will add more to the sale price than it costs. For a figure you can budget on, get at least three itemised quotes from licensed builders working to the same scope of works.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need council approval to renovate?

Painting, replacing fixtures and new flooring usually need no approval. Structural changes, extensions and anything that changes the building's footprint usually need council or certifier approval. Electrical and plumbing work must be done and certified by a licensed tradesperson. The rules are set by each state and run by local councils, so check with yours before booking trades.

Will a renovation increase my capital gains tax?

For an investment property, the cost of capital improvements is generally added to the asset's cost base, which reduces the capital gain when you sell. Your main residence is usually exempt from capital gains tax.
